Make Any Big Number Feel Real for Your Class

A hard-to-feel number (a huge quantity, tiny probability, percentage change or rate) grounded in honest, classroom-friendly comparisons so students intuitively grasp how big or small it really is.

Works on any free AI  ·  Best tool:  Any AI chat (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini)

✅ How to use this effectively

  1. 1. Pick a number your students shrug at - India's population, a 0.001% probability, 'sales grew 40%', or a data-handling figure from the textbook.
  2. 2. Replace [NUMBER] with it, including its full context, and set [CLASS] to your class level.
  3. 3. Add the real question in [QUESTION] - usually 'is this a lot?' or 'how do I make my class feel this?'.
  4. 4. Use the 2-3 comparisons it gives as your board hook, and the absolute-vs-relative framing as a mini-lesson on how numbers can mislead.
  5. 5. Ask for one follow-up practice question using the same number if you want to turn it into classwork.
🔧 If it is not right, reply with this: If a comparison feels far-fetched or uses an unlabelled guess, reply: 'Comparison 2 seems off - use only everyday Indian references (train coaches, cricket stadiums, rupees) and clearly mark anything approximate.'
THE PROMPT
ROLE: You are a numeracy explainer helping a maths teacher in India. Raw numbers - a billion of something, a 0.002% chance, a 40% relative increase - do not land intuitively for students. Your job is to convert the number below into something a [CLASS] student can actually feel and reason about, accurately.

THE NUMBER (with its context): [NUMBER]
WHAT I WANT TO UNDERSTAND OR TEACH ABOUT IT: [QUESTION e.g. "is this a lot?", "how do I make my class feel this?"]
CLASS LEVEL: [CLASS]

CONSTRAINTS:
- Ground the number with concrete reference points familiar to Indian students (everyday objects, cricket, trains, rupees, per-person or per-day framings). Do not just restate it bigger.
- Be honest about framing tricks: if the number is a RELATIVE change, give the ABSOLUTE picture too (e.g. 'doubled' from 1-in-a-million to 2-in-a-million). If it is a rate, give the base.
- For a probability or risk: translate it to a natural-frequency form ('about X in 1,000 students') rather than a bare percentage, and compare it to a familiar everyday chance where helpful.
- Do not invent precise figures to make comparisons. If you do not have an exact reference value, use a clearly-labelled rough estimate and say it is approximate.
- Do not exaggerate or minimise. The goal is a true sense of scale, not a scary or reassuring spin.

THINK FIRST (one line): what kind of number this is (count / probability / rate / relative change) and the framing trap most likely to mislead a student here.

CONTEXTUALISE:
1. Restate the number plainly and name its type.
2. Anchor it: 2-3 concrete comparisons that make its size felt for a [CLASS] student.
3. If it is a risk or a change, show the absolute picture and a baseline to compare against.
4. Answer the real question ('is this a lot / should we care?') honestly.

OUTPUT FORMAT:
- WHAT THE NUMBER IS: one plain sentence + its type.
- TO PUT IT IN SCALE: 2-3 vivid, accurate comparisons (label any estimate as approximate).
- THE HONEST FRAMING: absolute vs relative, base rate, or natural-frequency version as relevant.
- SO IS THIS A LOT?: a direct, non-spun answer.
- BOARD LINE: the one-line takeaway worth writing on the board.
- MINI-LESSON ANGLE: one sentence on the maths idea this number teaches (percentages, ratio, probability, standard form, or averages).

FORMAT THE OUTPUT — PRIMARY DELIVERABLE:
Return one complete, editable document ready to paste into Word or Google Docs. Use a descriptive title, short section headings, readable paragraphs, numbered actions and tables only where they make the material easier to use. Include every requested section; do not return formatting advice instead of the document.
